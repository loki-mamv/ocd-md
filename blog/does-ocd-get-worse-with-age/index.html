Does OCD Get Worse With Age?

October 12, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: not as a rule. OCD usually starts young and then rises and falls for years, and the longest follow-up studies show most people improve over the decades. Age itself does not drive symptoms upward. What makes OCD worse is time spent untreated, stress, and other health changes that arrive along the way. A first episode of compulsive behavior after midlife is uncommon and deserves a medical check.

The National Institute of Mental Health describes the pattern in one sentence: symptoms "may start slowly and can go away for a while or worsen as time passes" (NIMH). That leaves room for both outcomes. The research below shows which is more likely, and why.

When does OCD usually start?

Early. NIMH says symptoms usually begin between late childhood and young adulthood, and most people are diagnosed as young adults (NIMH). A meta-analysis of 192 epidemiological studies found that, for obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, 24.6% of cases began before age 14, 45.1% before age 18, and 64.0% before age 25. The peak age at onset was 14.5 years and the median was 19 (Solmi et al., 2022, Molecular Psychiatry).

For most people, "getting older with OCD" means living with a condition that began in the teens or twenties. The question is what happens to it across the next 40 or 50 years.

What happens to OCD over a lifetime?

On average it eases, though it often does not leave. A Swedish study re-examined people about 40 years after they were admitted for OCD, with a mean follow-up of 47 years from onset. Improvement was seen in 83%. The authors concluded that after several decades most people improve, although most continue to have clinical or subclinical symptoms (Skoog and Skoog, 1999, Archives of General Psychiatry).

The same study found that symptoms changed in kind for 58% of patients. A person may trade one theme for another over the years, which can feel like the disorder is getting worse when it is changing shape.

Treated adults do better in the medium term than many expect. A meta-analysis of 17 studies with 1,265 adults, followed for a mean of 4.91 years, found a pooled remission rate of 53% (Sharma et al., 2014, Journal of Clinical Psychiatry). Relapse is common, though. In the Brown Longitudinal Obsessive Compulsive Study, 59% of the adults who remitted over five years later relapsed (Eisen et al., 2013). Our article on whether OCD can go away on its own covers those remission numbers in detail.

Does untreated OCD get worse over time?

This is the part of the "worse with age" idea that holds up. The clock that matters is years without adequate treatment, not birthdays. A 2023 systematic review reported that the duration of untreated illness in OCD averages 87.5 to 94.5 months, longer than in schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. Across the eight studies it included, a longer untreated period went with a lower treatment response and greater symptom severity (Perris et al., 2023, Journal of Personalized Medicine).

An Italian study of 251 patients found a mean untreated period of 106.19 months. People untreated for more than 24 months had a poorer response to their first adequate medication trial (Albert et al., 2019, European Psychiatry). In the Brown study, a shorter duration of illness predicted remission (Eisen et al., 2013).

These studies show an association, and they cannot prove that delay causes the worse outcome. The pattern is consistent: rituals and avoidance that go unchallenged for years are harder to shift later.

What makes OCD flare up?

Flares tend to follow life events, and life events accumulate with age. That is one reason OCD can seem to worsen as people get older.

  • Stress. NIMH states that symptoms often get worse during times of stress (NIMH).
  • Hormonal and reproductive events. In a survey of women with OCD, 101 returned questionnaires. Of those, 49 reported worse symptoms in the premenstrual period, 17 during pregnancy, and 9 during menopause, while 11 reported improvement during pregnancy (Vulink et al., 2006). See our guide to postpartum OCD.
  • Trauma. In a Brazilian sample of 1,001 patients, posttraumatic stress disorder after age 40 was one of the factors linked to OCD that began at or after 40 (Frydman et al., 2014).
  • Depression. In a Swedish population study of older adults, depression was present in 34.6% of those with OCD, compared with 8.0% of those with no obsessions or compulsions (Klenfeldt et al., 2014). Our sister site depression.md covers how low mood is recognized and treated.

Can OCD start later in life?

Yes, but it is uncommon. In the Brazilian sample above, 8.6% of patients had late-onset OCD, defined as distress and interference beginning at or after age 40. It was more likely in women, in people with more than 10 years of milder subclinical symptoms, and around a recent pregnancy in the person or someone close to them (Frydman et al., 2014, Journal of Psychiatric Research).

That second factor matters. Much "late-onset" OCD is a long-standing mild tendency that crosses the line into a disorder after a major stressor, not something that appears from nowhere.

What does OCD look like in older adults?

OCD does not disappear in old age. A survey of 2,798 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and over found a 12-month prevalence of 1.5% (Grenier et al., 2009). A Swedish study of 900 people aged 70 and older found a one-month prevalence of 2.9%, and a further 21% had obsessive-compulsive symptoms below the diagnostic threshold (Klenfeldt et al., 2014, American Journal of Geriatric Psychiatry).

A literature review concluded that the domains and severity of OCD symptoms do not change with age, although pathologic doubt may worsen (Jazi and Asghar-Ali, 2020, Journal of Psychiatric Practice). A Dutch population study of 15,194 people found that checking behaviors drove most of the rise in provisional OCD diagnoses at older ages (Cath et al., 2017).

Older adults are also undertreated. In the Canadian survey, respondents with OCD had the lowest rate of health service use among those with anxiety or mood disorders. In an international clinic sample of 416 outpatients, 20.8% of patients aged 65 and over had received cognitive-behavioral therapy, compared with 41.8% of younger patients (Dell'Osso et al., 2017, European Psychiatry). The 2020 literature review names exposure and response prevention as the first-line treatment for older adults.

When are new compulsions in later life a warning sign?

When they are new, appear after midlife, and come with other changes. Compulsive behavior can be an early sign of frontotemporal dementia. In one clinic study, compulsive behaviors were presenting symptoms in 11 of 29 patients with frontotemporal dementia (38%), compared with 5 of 48 patients with Alzheimer's disease (10%). Repetitive checking was the most frequent (Mendez et al., 1997). In a later series of 87 patients with frontotemporal dementia, 49% had new or increased obsessive-compulsive behaviors and 29% had new hoarding (Mitchell et al., 2019, Cortex).

The international diagnostic criteria for the behavioural variant list perseverative or compulsive behaviours as one of six core features, alongside disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, changes in eating, and a decline in executive function (Rascovsky et al., 2011, Brain). New rituals in a person with no history of OCD, plus a change in personality or judgment, call for a medical evaluation. Most late-life OCD is not dementia, but this pattern should not be treated as anxiety alone.

The bottom line

OCD does not reliably get worse with age. It usually starts before 25, fluctuates, and tends to ease over decades, though most people keep some symptoms. What worsens the outlook is a long period without treatment, and the average delay runs to more than seven years. Flares cluster around stress, hormonal change, trauma, and depression. Treatment works in older adults too, and they receive it less often. A first episode of compulsive behavior in later life should prompt a visit to a doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age is OCD at its worst?

No single age is the worst for everyone. OCD most often begins in the teens or early twenties, with a peak age at onset of 14.5 years in a meta-analysis of 192 studies. Severity then rises and falls, and NIMH notes that symptoms often get worse during times of stress.

Can you develop OCD in your 40s or 50s?

Yes, though it is uncommon. In a Brazilian sample of 1,001 patients, 8.6% had OCD that began at or after age 40. Late onset was more likely in women and in people who had more than 10 years of milder symptoms beforehand.

Is OCD common in people over 65?

It is not rare. A survey of 2,798 adults aged 65 and over found a 12-month prevalence of 1.5%. A Swedish study of 900 people aged 70 and older found a one-month prevalence of 2.9%.

Can new OCD symptoms be a sign of dementia?

Sometimes. In one study, compulsive behaviors were presenting symptoms in 38% of patients with frontotemporal dementia, compared with 10% of patients with Alzheimer's disease. New rituals after midlife in a person with no OCD history, together with personality or judgment changes, should be checked by a doctor.

Does OCD treatment still work if you have had it for decades?

It can. A review of OCD in older adults names exposure and response prevention as the first-line treatment. Studies link a longer untreated period to a poorer response, so earlier treatment is better, but a long history does not rule out improvement.
