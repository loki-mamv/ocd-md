What Is Existential OCD?

The short answer: Existential OCD is a clinical label for obsessive-compulsive disorder whose obsessions are unanswerable questions about life, death, reality, or the self. The person cannot drop the questions, and spends hours analyzing, checking, or seeking reassurance about them. It is not a separate diagnosis in the DSM. It is ordinary OCD with an existential theme, and it is treated the same way: exposure and response prevention (ERP) therapy, SSRI medication, or both.

What does existential OCD look like day to day?

Psychologist Fred Penzel describes the pattern in an International OCD Foundation (IOCDF) article. He defines existential OCD as "intrusive, repetitive thinking about questions which cannot possibly be answered." The questions usually concern the meaning, purpose, or reality of life, the existence of the universe, or one's own existence. Some clinicians also call it "philosophical OCD."

Common themes include:

  • Meaning and purpose: "Why are we here? What is the point of anything if we all die?"
  • Death and being forgotten: repeated thoughts that one day you will be gone and no one will remember you.
  • Reality: "How do I know I am not dreaming, or in a coma, and my whole life is imaginary?"
  • Self and identity: "Is this really me? Are my feelings my own?"
  • Scale of the universe: fear that humans are tiny specks, so nothing matters.

The compulsions are mostly mental. People analyze the question for hours, test whether things "feel real," read philosophy or physics to find a final answer, or ask others to confirm that life has meaning. Penzel notes that any answer lasts a few minutes, then slips away when a new doubt appears. Because the rituals happen inside the head, existential OCD often overlaps with what people call "Pure O" OCD.

Is existential OCD an official diagnosis?

No. The DSM-5-TR lists obsessive-compulsive disorder, not themed subtypes. "Existential OCD" is a descriptive term used by clinicians and patient groups such as the IOCDF. A clinician would diagnose OCD and note the theme of the obsessions.

The diagnosis rests on the general OCD features. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) says people with OCD generally:

  • Cannot control their obsessions or compulsions, even when they know they are excessive.
  • Spend more than 1 hour a day on obsessions or compulsions.
  • Get no pleasure from compulsions, only brief relief from anxiety.
  • Have significant problems in daily life because of the symptoms.

An existential theme does not change these rules. What matters is the time, the distress, and the loop of doubt and mental ritual.

Be careful with search results here. A 2023 paper in Frontiers in Psychology uses the phrase "obsessive-compulsive existential type". That paper is a philosophical (phenomenological) model of OCD as a whole. It does not describe the existential-themed subtype, and it is not a treatment study.

How is it different from normal philosophical thinking?

Almost everyone wonders about death or meaning at some point. Penzel points out that students discuss these questions in philosophy or physics classes, then move on to other thoughts. The difference in OCD is not the question. It is what the question does to you.

  • Curiosity feels open. You can enjoy the question, set it aside, and come back later.
  • Existential OCD feels urgent. The question brings intense anxiety or dread, and you feel you must resolve it now.
  • Curiosity tolerates no answer. OCD demands certainty that the question cannot give.
  • Curiosity takes minutes. OCD can take hours a day and crowd out work, sleep, and relationships.

Penzel also notes that existential OCD is often missed. It can look like depressive rumination about a meaningless life. It is also sometimes misdiagnosed as generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). If worry spreads across many everyday topics, GAD may fit better; anxiety.md covers that condition. If one unanswerable question returns again and again, with rituals to neutralize it, OCD is more likely.

How does existential OCD relate to derealization?

Derealization is the sense that the world around you is unreal, dreamlike, or distant. Depersonalization is the sense of being detached from your own body or thoughts. Both can overlap with existential OCD, because the obsessions often concern whether the world or the self is real.

The two are not the same thing. In existential OCD, the core problem is a doubt ("What if none of this is real?") and the urge to check or argue it away. In derealization, the core problem is a perceptual feeling of unreality. The two can feed each other. Anxious, hyper-focused checking of "does this feel real?" can make ordinary perception feel strange.

Research on this overlap is limited. A 2019 study of 60 treatment-seeking OCD patients found that higher anxiety predicted higher depersonalization and derealization scores. OCD severity itself predicted dissociative amnesia, not depersonalization or derealization. Treat any strong link between the two as unproven. Persistent feelings of unreality deserve their own assessment by a clinician.

How is existential OCD treated?

There are no clinical trials that test treatment only in existential OCD. The evidence comes from OCD in general, and the IOCDF says the same approaches apply.

Exposure and response prevention (ERP). NIMH describes ERP as a specific type of cognitive behavioral therapy that reduces compulsions, even in people who do not respond well to medication. For existential themes, exposure targets the thought and the uncertainty. Examples from Penzel's IOCDF article include:

  • Listening to short recordings that state the feared idea, such as "Nothing is real."
  • Posting a note with the feared idea where you will see it.
  • Watching films or reading articles that bring on the thoughts.
  • Writing feared sentences many times a day until they become boring.

Response prevention means you stop the mental rituals. You do not argue with the thought, analyze it, or look up articles that disprove it. You also stop asking others for comfort, because reassurance keeps the OCD loop going. Penzel adds acceptance as a key step: accept that the questions have no final answer, and that you can live with them.

Medication. SSRIs are standard OCD medicines. MedlinePlus lists fluvoxamine as an SSRI used to treat OCD, and notes it may take several weeks or longer to feel the full benefit. MedlinePlus also notes that clomipramine has been shown to be effective for many people with OCD.

Combined care. A 2022 meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials (1,113 patients) found that ERP plus medication improved OCD symptoms more than medication alone. The gain also held better during follow-up.

When should you get help?

Consider an evaluation if existential questions take up more than an hour a day, cause strong distress, or interfere with work, school, or relationships. Also seek help if you avoid people, places, or media because they trigger the thoughts. Look for a therapist trained in ERP for OCD; the IOCDF keeps a directory of providers.

Existential obsessions can come with low mood and hopeless thoughts. NIMH notes that many people with OCD also have a mood or anxiety disorder. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. In an emergency, call 911.

The bottom line

Existential OCD is OCD that fixes on questions no one can answer: why we exist, what is real, and what happens at death. It is a clinical description, not a formal DSM diagnosis. What separates it from normal curiosity is the anxiety, the time lost, and the mental rituals used to chase certainty. Subtype-specific research is scarce, but ERP, SSRIs, and the combination work for OCD in general, and clinicians apply them to existential themes. The goal is not to find the answer. The goal is to live well without one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can existential OCD go away without treatment?

OCD symptoms can ease for a while and then return, often during stress. NIMH describes OCD as long-lasting, and MedlinePlus notes that a completely symptom-free period is unusual. Treatment with ERP, medication, or both helps most people manage symptoms.

Is it OCD or depression if I keep thinking life is meaningless?

Depressive rumination tends to come with low mood and loss of interest, and the thoughts feel like conclusions. Existential OCD centers on doubt and anxiety, with an urge to analyze or neutralize the question. The two can occur together, so a clinician should assess both.

Should I read philosophy to settle my existential questions?

For someone with existential OCD, searching for a final answer usually works as a compulsion. Any relief tends to last minutes before a new doubt appears. In ERP, a therapist may instead use reading as planned exposure, without the goal of finding certainty.

What does a therapist mean by agreeing with the thought?

In ERP for existential themes, a therapist may ask you to say or write that the feared idea could be true, for example that nothing is real. The aim is to build tolerance to anxiety and uncertainty. Over time the thought loses its impact and can even become boring.

Does existential OCD mean I am losing touch with reality?

Doubting whether things are real is different from believing they are not real. Most people with OCD recognize that their thoughts are excessive. If you have persistent feelings of unreality or unusual beliefs, ask a clinician for a full assessment.
